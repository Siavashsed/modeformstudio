**Title:** Do you budget around your best booking month or your worst one? I think most of us get this backwards

Something I wish someone had told me when I started taking freelance bookings: build your budget on your worst month, not your average one.

The averaging trap is sneaky. You have a strong fashion week, invoice a big number, and your brain quietly files it as your new normal. Then you take on fixed costs like rent, a lease or a stack of subscriptions sized for the good months. But invoiced is not paid. Showrooms and agencies regularly pay 60, 90, even 120 days out, so your best month on paper can reach your bank account in your slowest season.

I read a breakdown from a Paris stylist who invoiced €11,400 across four shows one September and signed for a flat on the strength of it. Then she waited 94 days for the biggest invoice to clear. Most of us get hurt in exactly that gap.

What actually helps: find your lowest-earning month from the last year or two and make your fixed costs fit inside it. Anything above that goes to buffer and taxes first. Spending comes last.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should freelance models and fashion stylists budget when their income changes so much from month to month?

Build your budget around your worst month of income, not your average one. Most freelancers in fashion do it the other way around, and that causes most of the money stress in this industry.

The reason is structural. Fashion work is seasonal and clustered. Fashion weeks, campaign seasons and showroom periods pack a lot of bookings into a few weeks, followed by long quiet stretches. On top of that, clients rarely pay when the work is done. Payment terms of 60 to 90 days are common, and late payment beyond that is normal. So your income has two layers of unevenness: when you earn it and when you actually receive it.

If you budget on your average, you will sign up for fixed costs (rent, a car, subscriptions, loan repayments) that your slow months cannot cover. Then you end up running on credit while you wait for invoices to clear.

A practical method:

1. Pull your actual deposits, not invoices, for the last 12 to 24 months. Find the lowest month.
2. Make your fixed costs fit inside that number. If they don't, that is the real problem to solve.
3. When a good month comes in, set aside tax first, then top up a cash buffer of at least three months of fixed costs. Only then spend.
4. Track outstanding invoices separately and never count them as money you have.
5. Where you can, ask for deposits or shorter terms, especially from new clients.

There's a good real-world example of this in a piece about a stylist who invoiced €11,400 in one Paris fashion month and had her biggest invoice paid 94 days late, after she had already committed to a new flat: https://siavashsed.github.io/modeformstudio/general-finance-tips-uneven-income/

The point is not to earn more in good months. It's to make sure the bad months can't hurt you.
