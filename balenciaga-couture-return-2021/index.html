Balenciaga's 2021 Couture Return Was the Wake-Up Call Fashion Needed

Balenciaga's return to haute couture in 2021 redefined luxury fashion's future. Here's why it mattered more than most people realized.

Why 52 Years Away Made the Return So Loaded

Cristóbal Balenciaga founded his couture house in 1937 and closed it in 1968, famously disgusted with the way ready-to-wear was eroding craft. The house never returned to the official couture calendar until Demna's July 2021 collection, Season 78. That gap is not just symbolic. It meant the brand was re-entering one of fashion's most gatekept institutions with a creative director known for irony, streetwear references, and deliberately ugly luxury. The tension was structural before a single look walked out.

What Demna actually showed was 52 looks, a pointed nod to those 52 missing years. The silhouettes were sculptural and severe, evening gowns with molded bodices, voluminous skirts built to architecture-level precision. But there were also hoodies rendered in duchess satin. A garbage bag, reproduced in black taffeta, twisted into a floor-length gown. The craftsmanship was undeniable. The references were deliberately uncomfortable. And that combination was the whole argument: couture isn't a sanctuary from culture, it's a mirror of it.

According to Vogue's runway review, the collection was described as a confrontation with fashion's own mythology. That read seems right to me. Demna wasn't paying homage to Cristóbal in any nostalgic sense. He was asking whether the values Cristóbal walked away from in 1968 had gotten better or worse.

The Industry Reaction Was Divided and That Division Reveals Something Real

The response split almost perfectly along generational lines, which is itself worth paying attention to. Older critics and couture traditionalists were either cautiously impressed by the technical execution or openly skeptical of the conceptual framing. Younger fashion writers and the brand's core audience were electrified. Social media engagement on Balenciaga's couture announcement posts ran into the millions within hours, and the brand's Instagram following surpassed 12 million by the end of that month.

Here's the thing... the people who dismissed this as stunt couture were missing the actual argument being made. The Business of Fashion noted at the time that couture's commercial relevance had been shrinking for decades, with the Fédération de la Haute Couture et de la Mode reporting that the number of official couture clients globally had hovered around 3,000 to 4,000 for years, a vanishingly small market. Balenciaga's return didn't try to fix that by appealing to that existing client base. It expanded what couture could mean to a global audience who would never buy it but cared intensely about it.

That's a different strategy than what most houses use couture for, which is heritage maintenance and press coverage. Demna was using it as cultural provocation, and whether you find that admirable or cynical probably tells you more about your own relationship to luxury than about the collection itself.

What This Meant for Modeling, Casting, and the Couture Body

I've spent enough time watching couture casting decisions to know that the bodies chosen for these shows are never accidental. Every measurement, every proportion, is a choice that encodes values. And Balenciaga's 2021 couture casting was, quietly, one of the more pointed things about the whole presentation. The lineup included models across a wider age range than most couture shows allow, and the styling consistently refused the kind of ethereal, otherworldly presentation that turns models into abstract objects.

Okay, I have to be honest here for a second. My friend Lena, who works in model casting in Paris, texted me during the show and said something like 'I cannot believe they put someone in a garbage bag gown and made it the most elegant thing on the runway.' And she was not wrong, but she also immediately added 'I wonder how long they spent on that hem.' Because that's the thing about couture: the joke, if it is a joke, still has to be flawless at the seam level. I once tried to explain to a non-fashion person why a badly finished hem on a $40,000 gown is more damaging to a house's reputation than a badly finished hem on a $40 dress, and they looked at me like I'd lost my mind. But that's the whole contract of couture, and Balenciaga honored it even while poking at everything around it.

The casting choices also fed into a broader 2021 conversation about who couture is actually made for and who gets to wear it symbolically, even in a show context. McKinsey's State of Fashion 2021 report had already flagged that consumers under 35 were applying unprecedented scrutiny to luxury brands' casting and representation decisions, with 67% of Gen Z respondents saying a brand's cultural values affected their purchase intent. Balenciaga's casting wasn't charity. It was strategy. But it was also, I'd argue, the right strategy for the right reasons.

The Lasting Argument: Couture as Cultural Criticism, Not Just Craft

The misconception I keep running into is that this return was primarily about commerce, that Balenciaga was trying to grab couture's prestige halo for commercial gain. That's too simple. Yes, brand equity matters. Yes, couture coverage drives aspirational marketing. But the deeper move here was about positioning couture as a space for serious cultural commentary rather than a relic kept alive by a handful of extremely wealthy clients ordering bespoke eveningwear.

In my work covering collections over the past several years, I've watched house after house treat couture as a museum piece, a demonstration of craft divorced from contemporary meaning. That approach produces beautiful clothes and very little cultural resonance. What Demna did in July 2021 was argue, through the work itself, that the highest level of fashion construction should be in direct conversation with the most uncomfortable parts of contemporary life. Wealth, waste, the grotesque theater of luxury in a post-pandemic world. The garbage bag gown wasn't a gimmick. It was a thesis.

And the craft backed the thesis up. That's the part that gets lost in the culture-war noise around Balenciaga generally. The atelier work on those 52 looks was, by multiple accounts from people who saw them up close, extraordinary. The New York Times reported on the construction techniques used, noting that the molded forms required techniques that hadn't been in active use at the house for decades and were revived specifically for this collection. That's not a stunt. That's a serious commitment to the form even while interrogating its assumptions. If you care about where fashion design is heading, that combination is exactly what you should want to see more of.

If you're a designer, a student, or anyone trying to understand what luxury fashion's next chapter looks like, go back and watch the Balenciaga Couture Season 78 show in full. Not for the spectacle, but to study how Demna held two contradictory ideas in tension at once: radical respect for craft and radical skepticism about what that craft has historically been used to say. That balance is genuinely hard to achieve, and it's rarer than the industry pretends. The next time you're evaluating whether a collection is doing something real or just performing novelty, ask yourself whether the construction supports the concept or whether the concept is papering over weak construction. In 2021, Balenciaga made sure you couldn't separate the two.
