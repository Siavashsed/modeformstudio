**Title:** Late agency payments cost me more than taxes ever did. What's your system for lumpy income?

I've freelanced in fashion for years, and the money lesson that stuck with me had nothing to do with rates or negotiating. It was about timing.

Back in 2019 an agency owed me $4,100 for a Paris showroom booking. It reached my account 117 days later. While I waited, I put two months of rent on a credit card at 24.99% APR. The interest came to about $190, which was basically a full day of fittings gone.

Two habits fixed it for me:

1. Every payment goes into a holding account first. I pay myself a fixed amount on a day I pick, whenever the checks happen to clear.
2. 30% of every deposit goes into a separate tax account the same day, before I touch anything else.

It's boring. But now a late agency payment is an annoyance and not a credit card bill, and quarterly estimates don't make me panic anymore.

How do the rest of you handle slow agency payments? Do you build a buffer first, or chase invoices harder?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should freelance models and fashion designers manage irregular income and save for taxes?

The short answer: keep the day you get paid separate from the day you pay yourself, and take tax money off the top of every deposit before you treat any of it as yours.

Fashion work pays in lumps. You get a big booking, then nothing for six weeks, then an agency invoice that clears months late. I once waited 117 days for $4,100 from a Paris showroom booking and paid my rent with a card at 24.99% APR in the meantime. That cost me about $190 in interest, roughly a day of fittings, on money I had already earned.

This is the system I recommend:

1. Use three accounts: a holding account where every client and agency payment lands, a tax account, and your normal checking account.

2. Move 30% of every deposit to the tax account on the day it arrives. In the US, self-employment tax alone is 15.3% before income tax, so 30% is a sensible starting point for many freelancers. Your real number depends on your bracket, your state and your deductions, so check it with an accountant once a year.

3. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Take a conservative monthly average of your last 12 months and move that amount from holding to checking on the same day every month. Good months build the buffer and slow months draw it down.

4. Keep two to three months of expenses in holding before you give yourself a raise. That buffer is what protects you when agencies pay late.

5. Pay your quarterly estimates from the tax account.
