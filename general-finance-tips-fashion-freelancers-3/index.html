Fashion Freelancers Lose Money Between Invoices, Not Between Seasons

General finance tips from a working fashion pro: a fixed salary, tax money you never touch, and a buffer sized for invoice income.

The finance habit that matters most is a boring one. Pay yourself a fixed salary from a separate account, and let every invoice land somewhere you can't spend it. The rest is decoration. In March 2023 I finished a styling job for a showroom in Paris and got a $4,200 transfer on a Tuesday. By Friday I'd spent about a third of it on a wool coat, a sample-sale haul and a dinner I can still taste. Then my quarterly tax estimate arrived, I was short by roughly $1,100, and I put it on a credit card charging 24%.

Money in fashion is lumpy. A fitting season pays like a lottery win, then six weeks of nothing follows. That swing is hard on anyone, but the baseline is already thin. The Federal Reserve's 2023 household well-being survey found that 37% of US adults couldn't cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent. Add irregular invoices to that and you get a field where talented people quit for money reasons, not creative ones. Here's what has actually worked for me.

Turn Lumpy Invoices Into a Paycheck You Can Predict

The fix is a multi-account system, and it's dull on purpose. Client money goes into a holding account. Twice a month, a fixed amount moves from there into your everyday checking. That's your pay. I set mine at $2,600 a month after looking at my three worst months of 2022, which averaged about $3,050. Paying myself from the low end meant the good months quietly built a cushion without any willpower.

Here's the split that finally stuck for me:

  • Holding account: every invoice lands here first, no exceptions.
  • Tax account: a set percentage moves out the same day the invoice clears.
  • Buffer account: whatever's left after your pay goes here until it hits your target.
  • Checking: only your fixed pay, so your daily spending has a hard ceiling.

It feels stingy at first. You'll resent it in month two. But I walk through the mechanics further in finance tips for fashion creatives who are tired of scrambling every month, and the pattern holds: people who pay themselves a salary stop making panic decisions about rates.

Treat Tax Money as Someone Else's Money

Self-employed workers pay a 15.3% self-employment tax for Social Security and Medicare, as the IRS explains, and that's before income tax. It's the number that ambushes stylists, models and pattern-makers who spent years watching an employer withhold everything. Federal income tax stacks on top, then your state. For more on this, see Fashion Freelancers Lose Thousands Every Season to One Tax Mistake.

My rule is 28% moved the same day an invoice clears. In most cases that's slightly too much, and I'd rather get a refund than a bill. It won't fit everyone, though. In a state with no income tax, or with heavy legitimate deductions, 22% might be plenty. Pay an accountant for one hour to tell you. I spent $180 on that in 2022 and it's the cheapest advice I've ever bought.

One misconception deserves killing. Write-offs aren't free money. A $900 camera lens might cut your tax bill by $250, which means you still spent $650 you didn't have to. I covered that trap in the finance tips every fashion professional actually needs, and I still see people buying gear in December just to "save on taxes."

Fund the Boring Buffer Before the Fun Stuff

Salaried people are told to keep three months of expenses in cash. My position is that anyone paid by invoice needs four to six. A $400 surprise is a Tuesday for a freelancer, and a ghosted $6,000 invoice is a season. The buffer isn't an investment. It's what lets you say no to a client who pays late and rude.

I'm not 100% sure six months is right for everyone, honestly. My friend Odile keeps four and swears by it, while I panicked and built mine to seven after a client sat on a $6,000 invoice for ten weeks. She thinks I'm ridiculous. I also once forgot about $1,400 in a savings account for eight months because I'd named it "Do Not Look." It worked a little too well. For more on this, see Finance Tips for Fashion Professionals Who Are Tired of Being Broke Between Seasons.

The trade-off is real. Cash in a buffer earns less than the market, and in a good year that gap costs you. Park it in a high-yield savings account and accept the drag. If your income swings with fashion weeks, finance tips for fashion professionals who are tired of being broke between seasons covers how to time the build.

Spend on Tools That Pay Back, Not on Looking Successful

Fashion rewards the appearance of money, and that's a trap. In 2021 I bought a $1,600 dress form I used exactly four times. That's $400 per use. Meanwhile, $14 a month for invoicing software that chased late payments recovered more than that in its first quarter.

So here's my filter. Before any purchase over $200, write down the specific invoice it will help you earn. If you can't name one, wait 14 days. Most wants die by day nine.

And pay down high-interest debt before you chase returns. A card charging 24% is a guaranteed loss that no investment will reliably beat. I learned that from my own Paris coat... and I'd rather you learn it from this paragraph. For more on this, see Fashion People Are Bad With Money. Here's How to Fix That..

Open a second bank account today and name it "Tax." Then move 28% of your most recent client payment into it before you spend another dollar, and set a recurring transfer so the next invoice does the same.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a freelancer set aside for taxes?

Most US freelancers should move 25 to 30% of each payment into a separate tax account. Self-employment tax alone is 15.3%, and income tax comes on top. An accountant can narrow the number for your state and deductions.

How big should an emergency fund be if my income is irregular?

Aim for four to six months of expenses, not the usual three. Invoice income can stall for weeks without warning, and a larger cushion lets you refuse bad clients.

Should I pay off debt or build savings first?

Build a small buffer of one month first, then attack any debt above roughly 10% interest. After that, grow the buffer to its full size. A card at 24% costs you more than savings will earn.
