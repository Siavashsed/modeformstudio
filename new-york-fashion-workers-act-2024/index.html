New York Just Gave Models Ownership of Their Digital Faces

New York's Fashion Workers Act, signed December 2024, caps agency commissions, forces payment in 45 days and bans unapproved AI digital replicas of models.

On December 19, 2024, Governor Kathy Hochul signed the Fashion Workers Act. It's the first law in the United States to treat a model's digital likeness as something an agency can't use, sell or hand to an AI vendor without the model's clear written consent. I booked my first editorial at 19 and spent two years of my twenties chasing a $2,400 check from a Midtown agency. It finally showed up eleven months late, with a $310 "administrative fee" deducted that nobody could ever explain. So when the bill cleared Albany, I texted three former bookers before I'd even finished reading it. My view is that this is the most important thing to happen to modeling labor in a generation, and the AI clause is the part people will still be arguing about in 2030.

Look at the timing. In July, Mango launched a campaign for its teen line using fully AI-generated models, and Reuters covered it as a sign of where retail was heading. A year earlier, McKinsey estimated generative AI could add between $150 billion and $275 billion to apparel, fashion and luxury operating profits within three to five years. Money on that scale doesn't wait for anyone's permission. New York moved first, and the rest of the industry is now reacting to it.

What the Fashion Workers Act Actually Requires

The bill (S2477 in the New York State Senate) was pushed for years by the Model Alliance and its founder, Sara Ziff. It closes a loophole that let modeling agencies call themselves "management companies." That label kept them out from under New York's employment agency rules for decades.

Here's what changes once it takes effect in June 2025:

  • Agencies have to register with the state Department of Labor.
  • Models get paid within 45 days of the agency receiving payment from the client.
  • Commissions are capped at 20% of the model's earnings.
  • Agencies can't create, alter or license a digital replica without separate, specific written consent.
  • Contracts can't auto-renew without the model's agreement.

None of this sounds radical. That's what should make you angry. Most of it would be basic decency in any other job.

Why the Digital Replica Clause Matters More Than the Payment Rules

The 45-day payment rule will change more lives in the short term. I'll admit that. But the digital replica language is the part with real teeth over the long run, because a scan of your body done once can be reused forever. A late check is a single injury. Your likeness ending up in some brand's asset library is an injury that keeps happening.

The consent problem nobody solved before

When I worked as a casting consultant in 2023, I sat in on two fittings where the client wanted a full-body 3D scan "for fit purposes." Both times the model signed a release on an iPad without reading it. Nobody in the room, me included, could have told you whether that scan would show up in an e-commerce render six months later. Under the new law, a buried release clause doesn't count as consent. It has to be explicit and separate, and it has to say what the replica will be used for.

And here's the thing... the law only covers what agencies do. A brand that scans a model directly, or pays a vendor to build a synthetic face from scratch, sits outside a lot of it. That's a real gap, and it's the honest objection to calling this a finished fix.

The Pushback, and Where Critics Have a Point

Some agencies argued the 20% cap would push smaller boutiques out of business, since many of them front travel, test shoots and apartment costs for new faces. I don't think that argument holds up. The good boutiques I know already charge around 20% and recoup development costs openly. The ones that'll struggle are the ones that made their money on vague deductions.

But there's one critique I can't wave away. Synthetic models made from nothing, like the Mango campaign, don't replicate any real person. So no consent is needed and no model gets paid. The law protects your face. It doesn't protect your job from a face that never existed.

Okay, a small confession. My friend Inès, who did catalog work in Paris for years, tried one of those AI "model generator" apps in October just to see how close it could get to her. Honestly, it gave her a slightly cross-eyed cousin with six-and-a-half fingers, and we laughed about it for a solid ten minutes. Then she went quiet and said, "Give it eighteen months." I'm not 100% sure she's right about the timeline. I'm also not sure she's wrong, and I've been thinking about it every week since. What 2024 Taught the Industry About Regulation

2024 was the year fashion found out the regulators weren't bluffing. Europe spent it pushing ahead with traceability rules, which I wrote about in why fashion was not ready for the EU Digital Product Passport, and the supply chain pressure behind it is covered in how the Digital Product Passport is reshaping fashion's supply chain. The New York law is the labor side of the same shift. People who used to be taken on trust are now accountable in writing.

I've seen this pattern in every corner of the business I've worked in. Self-regulation lasts right up until somebody passes a law, and then everyone claims they supported it all along. Expect agencies to start advertising "FWA compliant" contracts by spring as if it were their own idea.

So what about you, if you're a working model or managing one? Payment rules only help if you track your money. A 45-day deadline is useless if you don't know when the client paid. If tracking invoices feels like a foreign language, start with this guide on fixing your money habits as a fashion professional. Most models I know have never kept a spreadsheet of their own bookings... and that's exactly the gap bad agencies have always exploited.

Do this tonight: open every agency contract and release you've signed in the past three years and search for the words "likeness," "image rights," "scan" and "digital." Write down every clause that gives anyone perpetual or unlimited use of your image. Then email your agency asking, in writing, which existing scans or replicas of you they hold and who they've been shared with. Their answer, or their silence, tells you what to fix before the law takes effect in June.

Frequently asked questions

When does the New York Fashion Workers Act take effect?

It was signed on December 19, 2024 and takes effect in June 2025, 180 days after signing. Agencies need to register with the New York Department of Labor and meet the payment and consent rules from that date.

Can a modeling agency use AI to make a digital version of me?

Not in New York without your clear, separate written consent that says how the replica will be used. A general release clause buried in a booking contract doesn't meet that standard.

What is the maximum commission a New York modeling agency can take?

The law caps commission at 20% of a model's earnings. It also bans many of the unexplained fees and deductions agencies used to take out of model pay.
