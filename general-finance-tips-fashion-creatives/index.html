The $6,840 Tax Bill That Rewrote My Money Rules

General finance tips for fashion creatives and models: tax accounts, dead-season cash buffers, and late payments, from a designer who learned it the hard way.

The finance advice that actually changes anything for people in fashion is boring. Move a fixed slice of every payment into a separate tax account the same day it lands, and build your cash cushion around your slowest season instead of your best one. I learned that the expensive way in April 2019, sitting on the floor of my Brooklyn studio with a shoebox of receipts and a $6,840 tax bill I'd been pretending wasn't coming. I'd just had my best year as a pattern cutter and freelance fit consultant, and I'd spent it like someone with a salary. Most general finance tips fail creative workers because they're written for steady paychecks, and almost nobody in this industry has one.

If that sounds familiar, you're in good company. The Federal Reserve's 2024 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking found that 63% of US adults would cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent, so more than a third of people couldn't. Now picture a model waiting 90 days on an agency check, or a designer fronting $2,300 in fabric for a sample run. The gap between earning money and actually receiving it is where fashion finances break, and everything below is aimed at that gap.

Pay the Tax Account Before You Pay Yourself

When you're self-employed in the US, you owe income tax plus a 15.3% self-employment tax covering Social Security and Medicare, according to the IRS. Nobody withholds it for you. So the money sitting in your checking account after a good shoot isn't really yours yet, and treating it like it is was exactly my 2019 mistake.

Why I use 30% and not 25%

Plenty of accountants will tell you 25% is enough. For lower earners it often is. But I've seen this pattern with at least a dozen freelancers I've worked alongside: the ones who saved 25% almost always came up short once state tax hit, and the ones who saved 30% usually got a small, pleasant surprise in April. I'd rather overshoot and move the leftover into savings than scramble.

Then pay it quarterly. The IRS expects estimated tax payments four times a year if you'll owe $1,000 or more, and skipping them adds penalties to a bill that already stings.

Size Your Emergency Fund to Your Dead Season

The standard line says three to six months of expenses. That's fine for an office job. For you, it's a guess pulled from someone else's life.

A better number comes from your own history. Here's how I worked mine out:

  • Pull every deposit from the last 24 months into one spreadsheet.
  • Find the longest stretch where income fell below your basic monthly costs.
  • Add up what you were short across that stretch.
  • Multiply that shortfall by 1.5, because the next drought is rarely kinder than the last.

For me, January through early March was brutal every single year, about 10 weeks of almost nothing after holiday campaigns wrapped. My target ended up near $11,000, which felt absurd until I hit it and slept better. Chase Late Payments Like It's Part of the Job

Late payment is the quiet killer here. Models especially have dealt with agencies sitting on earnings for months, which is part of why New York passed the Fashion Workers Act, championed by the Model Alliance, requiring payment within 45 days of completed work starting in June 2025. It's a real step. It also doesn't cover everyone, and a law only helps if you're willing to invoke it.

I'm honestly not sure there's one right way to chase money without wrecking a relationship. My friend Inès tried sending reminders as cheerful little GIFs, and honestly... it worked on two clients and deeply confused a third. And I once sent a stern "second notice" to a brand that had paid me early, because I'd misread my own spreadsheet. I still wince about that one. So maybe my system isn't perfect, but it's better than waiting and hoping.

What does work, at least in my experience: put payment terms and a late fee on every invoice, send a polite nudge on day one past due, and call on day 15. Most of the time, the call is what gets the money moving.

Stop Budgeting Like a Salaried Person

A common misconception is that a better budgeting app will fix irregular income. It won't. Apps track spending well, but they can't smooth out a $9,000 month followed by a $600 one.

Pay yourself a fake salary

What fixed it for me was a holding account. Every client payment lands there, the 30% tax cut leaves immediately, and I pay myself the same fixed amount on the 1st and 15th, no matter what came in. Good months refill the holding account, and lean months draw it down. Your life feels steady even when your income isn't.

It takes a few months to build enough of a float to make this work, and the first stretch is uncomfortable... you'll be paying yourself less than you're used to. But it's the single habit I'd keep if I had to drop everything else. Before you close this tab, open a second savings account and name it "Tax. Do Not Touch." Then go back to your most recent payment and transfer 30% of it in there right now. It takes about ten minutes, and it's the move that would have saved me $6,840 worth of panic.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a freelance model or designer save for taxes?

Set aside 25% to 30% of every payment the day it arrives, in a separate account. With the 15.3% self-employment tax on top of income tax, 30% is the safer number if you live in a state with income tax.

How big should my emergency fund be if my income is seasonal?

Look at your last two years and find your longest low-income stretch, then add up how short you were across it. Multiply that by 1.5 and make that your target, rather than a generic three to six months.

What can I do when an agency or client pays late?

Put payment terms and a late fee on every invoice, send a reminder the first day it's overdue, and call by day 15. In New York, the Fashion Workers Act requires model payments within 45 days, which gives you legal grounds to push.
