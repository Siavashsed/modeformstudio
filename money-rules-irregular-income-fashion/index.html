The Money Rules I Wish Someone Told Me Before My First Fashion Week

Real finance tips for people with irregular income, from a fashion pro who learned the hard way. Budgeting, taxes, and saving when your paychecks jump around.

If your income swings hard from month to month, the single best thing you can do is pay yourself a flat salary from a buffer account, not from whatever landed this week. That one move fixed more of my money stress than any budgeting app ever did. Back in the spring of 2019, I booked three shows in nine days, saw around $6,200 hit my account, and treated it like a windfall. New boots, a celebratory dinner, the works. By August I was borrowing rent money from my sister and eating a lot of lentils. That gap between a fat month and a lean one is where most creative careers quietly go broke.

Here's the position I'll defend all day: budgeting advice written for salaried office workers is close to useless for anyone in fashion, modeling, styling, or freelance design. Your problem isn't that you spend too much on coffee. Your problem is timing. Money arrives in unpredictable clumps, and the standard advice assumes a steady drip. So the fixes have to be different too.

Pay Yourself Last, Not First

Everyone repeats "pay yourself first." For irregular income I flip it. When a check clears, it goes into a holding account. Nothing touches it right away. Then on the 1st and the 15th, I transfer a fixed amount to my spending account, the same number every time, whether last week was a blowout or a drought. That fixed number becomes my fake salary.

I set mine at roughly the average of my six leanest months, not my best ones. That felt painful at first. But it meant I stopped riding the emotional roller coaster of feeling rich in March and terrified in July. When I tested this properly in 2021, I went a full year without a single "how am I paying rent" panic, which had literally never happened before.

The buffer is the whole game. Most of the time you want at least two months of that fake salary sitting in the holding account before you smooth things out. It's boring. It works. And it's the exact thing I keep telling younger models who assume a good season means the good times are permanent.

Taxes Will Ambush You If You Let Them

Nobody withholds taxes from a styling gig or a runway booking. That's your job now, and the bill is bigger than people expect because you're often paying self-employment tax on top of income tax. I've seen this pattern wreck people who had a genuinely great year and then owed thousands they'd already spent.

My rule is simple. Every payment that comes in, I move 25 to 30 percent straight into a separate tax account and pretend it doesn't exist. Some years I over-save and get a little back. Fine. I'd rather that than the alternative. The IRS actually expects most self-employed people to pay quarterly, and skipping those payments can trigger penalties, which you can read about straight from the IRS Self-Employed Tax Center.

If your finances feel messy across seasons, I've written more about the tax and cash-flow traps specific to this industry in the finance habits most fashion professionals ignore. Read it before your next big invoice, not after.

Build the Ugly Emergency Fund Before the Fun Investments

I love talking about investing. But an emergency fund comes first, and honestly most people in creative work don't have one. According to the Federal Reserve's 2023 survey, only about 63 percent of adults said they could cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent, per the Federal Reserve Economic Well-Being report. For people with lumpy income, being on the wrong side of that number is genuinely dangerous.

So my friend Renata tried the whole "invest everything, skip the savings" thing because some finance guy on TikTok told her cash was for losers, and honestly... it went about how you'd guess. Her laptop died right before a big campaign, she had no cushion, and she ended up selling a couple of investments at a loss just to eat that month. I felt bad even saying I told you so. I'm not 100 percent sure a six-month fund is right for everyone, and if you have wildly steady bookings maybe three is plenty, but going in with zero is the version that actually bites. For more on this, see The Money Habit That Saved My First Fashion Collection.

A separate survey from Bankrate has found year after year that a large share of Americans have more credit card debt than emergency savings. Don't be that statistic. Boring cash in a high-yield savings account is your seatbelt.

Separate Your Money Into Jobs, Not Just Accounts

The mistake I made for years was keeping everything in one checking account and trying to do math in my head. It never worked. Now I run a few accounts, each with one job, and I'd argue this is the highest-leverage change most freelancers can make in an afternoon.

Here's roughly how mine splits out:

  • Holding account: where all income lands first, untouched
  • Spending account: receives my fixed twice-monthly transfer
  • Tax account: 25 to 30 percent of everything, off limits
  • Emergency fund: high-yield savings, only for real emergencies

When the money is physically in different buckets, you stop accidentally spending your tax bill on a flight. If you want the version of this built specifically around seasonal fashion work, I broke it down in staying solvent between seasons and again for people running on empty in the creative's guide to stopping the monthly scramble.

Do one thing today. Open a second savings account, name it "taxes," and set an automatic transfer that moves 25 percent of your next payment into it the moment it clears. That's it. You'll thank yourself next April, and you'll have started the exact system that got me off the feast-and-famine treadmill for good.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I save for taxes if I'm a freelance model or stylist?

Set aside 25 to 30 percent of every payment in a separate account. If you earn more or live in a high-tax state, lean toward 30. The IRS expects most self-employed people to pay this quarterly, so don't wait until April.

What if my income is too unpredictable to budget at all?

That's exactly when the fake-salary method helps most. Route all income into one holding account, then pay yourself a fixed amount twice a month based on your leanest months. The buffer smooths out the chaos so your spending stops tracking your booking calendar.

Should I invest before I have an emergency fund?

No. Build at least three to six months of expenses in cash first. Investments can drop in value or take days to sell, and forced selling during a bad month often means locking in a loss right when you need the money.
