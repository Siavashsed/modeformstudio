**Title:** How do you handle money when one shoot pays more than a few months of bills, and then nothing comes in for weeks?

Budgeting has been the hardest part of freelancing in fashion for me. A normal monthly budget assumes the same amount lands every two weeks, and that has never been true in this industry.

In March 2022 I was paid $6,400 for a three-day lookbook shoot in Lyon. By the following Friday I'd spent about $2,000 of it on a wool coat I'd wanted for years. Then the April quarterly bill showed up and I was scrambling to cover it.

What fixed it wasn't a tighter budget. It was splitting my money across three separate accounts. Every payment gets divided the day it arrives: one account for taxes, one for rent and fixed bills, and one for spending and gear. The money has a job before I get a chance to treat it as a windfall.

The bigger change is that a slow month no longer feels like an emergency. The bills account pays me a steady amount whatever the shoots did that month.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What are the best general finance tips for freelancers in fashion or modeling who have irregular income?

The main thing to understand is that most general finance advice is written for people with a steady paycheck, and fashion work rarely pays that way. You might get a large payment for a shoot or a commission, then nothing for several weeks. A single monthly budget breaks down under that pattern.

The tip that has helped me most is to build your system around lumpy income, starting with three separate bank accounts.

1. A tax account. Every time you're paid, move a set percentage here immediately. Treat it as money you never had. This is what keeps a quarterly tax bill from becoming a crisis.

2. A bills account. This holds rent, insurance, subscriptions and other fixed costs. Fund it so that it pays you a steady, predictable amount each month, regardless of how many jobs you booked.

3. A spending and gear account. This is for everything discretionary, including the things that feel like rewards after a good job. When it's empty, it's empty.

I learned this the hard way. In March 2022 I was paid $6,400 for a three-day lookbook shoot in Lyon, and within a week I'd spent about $2,000 of it on a wool coat I'd wanted for years. Then the April quarterly bill arrived and I wasn't ready for it. The problem wasn't the coat itself. The problem was that the money had no job when it landed.

The rule that makes this work is to split every payment the day it arrives, before you decide what to do with it. That removes the willpower from the process.
