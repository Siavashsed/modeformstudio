**Title:** Anyone else get burned by mixing personal and business money as a designer?

A few years ago I was standing in a fabric shop in Lyon at 9 a.m. when my card was declined on a 1,400 euro order of wool crepe. My rent had cleared from the same account an hour earlier. I had a full spring collection planned and almost nothing left to cover it.

The lesson wasn't really about budgeting. It was that I was running one account as both my business and my life, so I could never tell what the business actually had. Every purchase felt like a guess.

What fixed it for me:

1. A separate business account, even if it's a basic one.
2. Paying myself a fixed amount on a schedule, like a salary.
3. A cash reserve for fabric and sampling before any sketch gets made. Materials and pattern work are paid for long before a single piece sells.
4. Pricing every design from real costs, not from what feels fair.

The timing gap is what kills many small labels. You pay for sampling and production months before the revenue arrives, so a profitable collection can still leave you short on cash.

How do you handle the gap between paying for materials and getting paid? Do you keep a set buffer, or take pre-orders to fund production?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should a fashion designer manage money and cash flow when starting a clothing brand?

The most effective first step is to stop using one bank account for both your business and your personal life. Most cash problems in fashion start there.

I learned this in March 2021, standing in a fabric shop in Lyon at 9 a.m. My card was declined on a 1,400 euro order of wool crepe, because my rent had cleared from the same account an hour earlier. I had a whole spring collection planned and no clear picture of what the business could actually spend.

Here is what I would do in order:

1. Separate the accounts. Open a dedicated business account and move every business expense and sale through it. This alone shows you what the brand really earns and costs.

2. Pay yourself on a schedule. Treat yourself like an employee with a fixed transfer on a fixed date. It stops you dipping into materials money for personal bills, and the reverse.

3. Understand the timing gap. Fashion is front-loaded. You pay for fabric, trims, pattern making, sampling and production months before customers pay you. A collection can be profitable on paper and still leave you unable to fund the next one.

4. Build a materials reserve before you sketch. Decide how much cash you need to cover the full production cycle, and don't start a collection you can't finance.

5. Price from real costs. Include fabric waste, sampling, labor, shipping and returns, then add margin. Many designers underprice because they price from feeling rather than numbers.

6. Consider pre-orders or small batches to bring revenue forward and reduce the amount you need to front.
