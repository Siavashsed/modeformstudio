Your Fashion Income Isn't Broken. Your Cash Flow Is.

General finance tips from a fashion insider: irregular income, a two-account system, and the tax bill that catches designers and models off guard.

The best general finance tip for anyone in fashion is boring: stop budgeting against your income and start budgeting against your lowest month. In March 2022 I closed a fitting season that paid $9,400 in three weeks, and I spent like it would happen again every month. By June I was paying a $612 rent bill with a credit card, standing in a Brooklyn fabric shop, wondering where it all went.

That wasn't a spending problem. It was a timing problem, and most of us in design, styling and modeling have the same one. Money arrives in lumps, tied to shoots, collections and fashion weeks, while rent shows up on the first like clockwork. So the usual advice, built for a salaried person with a predictable paycheck, falls apart fast. Here's what I do instead.

Build Around Your Worst Month, Not Your Best One

Pull your last 12 months of deposits and find the lowest one. That number is your real income. Anything above it is a bonus you haven't earned yet, and it should be treated that way.

This matters more than people admit. The Federal Reserve's Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households report found that only 63 percent of American adults would cover a $400 emergency with cash in 2023. Freelancers in fashion sit in the shakier part of that statistic, because one cancelled campaign can erase a quarter of a year.

If you want the longer version of this idea, I wrote about it in finance tips for fashion professionals who are broke between seasons. The short version is a buffer of three months of bare-minimum expenses, not your aspirational lifestyle expenses. Mine is about $4,100 a month, and I'm slightly embarrassed by how much of that is coffee and subway fare.

The Two-Account System That Actually Survives Fashion Week

Every payment that lands in my business account gets split within 48 hours. No exceptions, even when I'm exhausted and backstage at 11 p.m. The mechanics are simple, and that's why they work. For more on this, see Your Fashion Income Is Lumpy, So Your Budget Can't Be Flat.

What goes where

  • Tax account: 25 to 30 percent of every deposit, untouched until quarterly payments are due.
  • Operating account: the rest, from which I pay myself a fixed "salary" on the 1st and 15th.

That fixed salary is the trick. It turns lumpy income into something your brain can plan around. In my experience, the first three months feel like a pay cut, and then you stop noticing.

Why the tax split isn't optional

Self-employed people owe a 15.3 percent self-employment tax on top of regular income tax, according to the IRS. Plenty of first-year freelance models and stylists learn this in April. I learned it at 26, with a $3,800 bill and about $900 in savings.

The Money Mistakes Creatives Make While Feeling Responsible

Here's an opinion some people won't like: buying the good sewing machine, the studio space or the portfolio shoot is not automatically an investment. It's an expense with a story attached. Real investment has a number on the other end, a client, a contract, a measurable return.

I once spent $2,300 on a lookbook shoot for a collection I never produced. Nobody ever saw it. I called it "brand building" for a full year, which is a generous name for a very pretty mistake.

My friend Tessa did something sillier. She put every freelance check on a credit card for the points, planning to pay it off monthly, then lost track during a Milan trip and paid about $340 in interest on a 0 percent promo she'd forgotten expired. I'm not 100% sure the points scheme works for anyone with a chaotic calendar, and honestly I stopped trying after hearing that story. Maybe you're more organized than we are. Probably. For more on this, see Why Your Fashion Income Needs Three Bank Accounts, Not One Budget.

The pattern I've seen again and again is that the people who stay solvent aren't the most frugal. They're the ones who look at their numbers weekly, even briefly. If that's a struggle, these finance tips for fashion creatives who scramble every month cover a simple Friday review that takes ten minutes.

Pricing Is a Finance Skill, Not a Confidence Exercise

Most money problems in this industry start at the quote. If you don't know your cost per hour, you can't know whether a $600 day rate is good or a quiet loss. Divide your target annual income by your realistic billable hours, which is usually around 1,100 to 1,300 a year once admin, fittings and unpaid pitching are subtracted. Then price from there.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks fashion designer pay, and the gap between salaried and self-employed outcomes is wide. Take the median as a reference point, not a promise. Freelancers also carry their own insurance, software and downtime, so your rate has to be meaningfully higher than a salaried equivalent.

One thing doesn't always work: raising rates across the board overnight. I tried that with three long-term clients in 2023 and lost one. The other two accepted without a word. So raise rates with new clients first, and with old ones at renewal. And if you want more on the broader habits, the finance tips fashion professionals actually need and mostly ignore pick up where this leaves off.

Open your bank app today, find your lowest-earning month from the past year, and set up an automatic transfer that moves 25 percent of every deposit into a separate savings account labeled "taxes." It takes about eight minutes, and it fixes the single biggest cash shock you'll face this year. For more on this, see Your Income Is Lumpy, So Your Budget Can't Be Monthly.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a freelance fashion designer save for taxes?

Set aside 25 to 30 percent of every payment in a separate account. That covers federal income tax plus the 15.3 percent self-employment tax for most people, though your state and bracket may push it higher.

How big should an emergency fund be with irregular income?

Aim for three months of bare-minimum expenses, and six if your work is seasonal or tied to a few clients. Base it on your lowest earning month, not your average.

Is it worth paying for a business accountant as a freelancer?

In most cases yes, once you earn more than around $40,000 a year or have multiple income streams. A good one typically finds deductions that cover their fee, and they stop you from missing quarterly deadlines.
