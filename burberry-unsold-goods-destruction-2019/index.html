Burberry Burned £28 Million of Stock and Fashion Finally Had to Answer for It

Burberry's 2019 backlash over destroying unsold goods forced a real reckoning in fashion. Here's what actually changed and what didn't.

Burberry admitted in its 2018 annual report that it had incinerated £28.6 million worth of unsold clothing, accessories, and perfume in a single year. The story broke properly in the summer of 2018 but the fallout, the legislation, the industry-wide scrambling, that all happened through 2019 and it hasn't really stopped since. I was sitting in a showroom in Paris in January 2019 when a buyer I'd worked with for years tossed a printed copy of the Reuters story across the table and said, half-joking, 'So are we all just burning things now?' Nobody laughed. The room got uncomfortable in a way that told you something had shifted.

What made Burberry's disclosure so destabilizing wasn't just the number, though £28.6 million is a lot of cashmere to set on fire. It was the casualness of it. Destroying unsold goods to protect brand exclusivity is standard practice across luxury. Burberry just happened to report it transparently, and suddenly the entire industry was staring at a habit it had never had to publicly defend before. By mid-2019, France had passed a law banning it outright. The ripple effects are still being felt in supply chain policy today.

Why Luxury Brands Have Always Done This

The logic isn't difficult to follow, even if it's hard to defend. A trench coat retailing at £1,800 loses most of its value the second it appears on a discount rack. Luxury positioning depends on scarcity, and scarcity depends on inventory control. For decades, the accepted industry answer to overstock was simple: destroy it, write it off, move on. No secondary market, no grey-market resellers, no dilution.

Burberry wasn't an outlier. Reuters reported at the time that the practice was widespread across luxury houses, with Burberry's transparency actually making it the example rather than the exception. Richemont, the Swiss group behind Cartier and IWC, had reportedly crushed and melted down watches worth hundreds of millions rather than allow them into unauthorized channels. The difference was that Richemont didn't disclose it in a public filing.

The real problem in 2019 wasn't that brands were doing this. It was that consumers, now armed with social media and a sharper sense of environmental cost, decided they were no longer willing to pretend it was acceptable. That shift in consumer tolerance is what turned a financial disclosure into a full crisis.

France Moved First and the Law Was Specific

In February 2019, France's government introduced the AGEC law, the Anti-Waste for a Circular Economy bill, which explicitly targeted the destruction of unsold non-food goods. By January 2020 it had passed, but the industry lobbying and public debate happened squarely in 2019, and designers across Paris knew what was coming. The law would eventually ban the destruction of unsold textiles, clothing, and footwear entirely, with fines for violations.

This was genuinely unprecedented. France was the first major economy to legislate against the practice directly, and the fashion industry's response was a mix of genuine reform and a fair amount of performative compliance. France's Ministry of Ecological Transition estimated that French companies destroyed or discarded around 630 million euros worth of unsold goods annually across sectors, with textiles making up a significant slice of that figure.

The broader significance here is something I'd argue gets missed in most coverage of this period. France's law wasn't just environmental policy. It was a direct intervention in luxury brand strategy, telling private companies they could not use destruction as a competitive tool. That's a more radical position than it sounds, and it set a precedent that the EU has since built on. The work that led to what we now know as the EU's digital product passport requirements, which you can read about in detail in this piece on how the EU Digital Product Passport changed fashion's supply chain, traces a direct line back to the political momentum that Burberry's 2019 backlash created.

Burberry's Response and What It Actually Did

Burberry moved quickly once the story had traction. By September 2018 they had announced a commitment to stop the practice, and through 2019 they published more detail on how they intended to redirect surplus stock, including partnerships with charities and material recycling programs. CEO Marco Gobbetti framed it as part of a broader sustainability push. The brand's 2019 responsibility report outlined targets around raw material sourcing and waste, though the language around timelines was loose in places.

Here's the thing... I'm genuinely uncertain how much of this was substantive versus damage control, and I think that ambiguity is honest. Burberry did stop publicly reporting destruction figures, which could mean they stopped doing it or could mean they just stopped reporting it. The lack of third-party verification in 2019 made it almost impossible to know. Business of Fashion covered the announcement with appropriate skepticism, noting that the commitment was voluntary and unverified at the time.

My friend Clara, who does sourcing for a mid-tier European accessories label, called me around that time and said she'd been told by a logistics contact that at least two other houses had quietly shifted from incineration to shredding, technically not burning, and were treating that as compliance with the spirit of the incoming rules. I have no idea if that's true. But it's the kind of thing you hear, and it tells you something about how the industry processes regulatory pressure: slowly, reluctantly, and always looking for the gap in the fence.

What 2019 Actually Changed for Designers and Buyers

The Burberry story pushed sustainability from a marketing talking point to a sourcing and production question that buyers started actually asking at trade shows. I saw this directly at Première Vision in Paris in September 2019. Stands that had never mentioned material origins were suddenly displaying certifications. Brands that had ignored the circular economy conversation for years were handing out literature about take-back schemes. Some of it was real. A lot of it was paper.

But the pressure on overproduction as a business model was becoming harder to ignore. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation's 'A New Textiles Economy' report, published in 2017 but widely cited throughout 2019 in response to the Burberry story, estimated that less than 1% of clothing material was recycled into new clothing, and that the total material value lost every year from inadequate recycling was worth more than $100 billion globally. That number got a lot of airtime in 2019.

The French Fashion Pact signed in August 2019 was the other major marker of how seriously the industry had started taking this kind of scrutiny. Sixty-plus brands, representing about a third of global fashion production, signed commitments around climate, biodiversity, and oceans. Whether those commitments hold is a separate question. But the fact that they felt necessary, that executives were willing to put their names on public targets, tells you how much the Burberry story had changed the political temperature inside the industry in the space of roughly twelve months.

The practical shift for designers and buyers was this: inventory management started being treated as a reputational risk, not just a financial one. Overproduction had always been a margin problem. After 2019, it was also a front-page problem. That changes the conversation in a boardroom in a way that sustainability reports alone never managed to.

  • Brands began auditing destruction clauses in supplier contracts
  • Buyers started asking about end-of-season stock protocols at order stage
  • Some houses quietly introduced tiered markdown authorization rather than default destruction for aged inventory

None of this happened fast. Most of it happened unevenly. But the direction, at least, was set. And the EU's subsequent regulatory work, including the product transparency frameworks that are now being built out and that are covered in depth in this article on why the fashion industry wasn't ready for the EU Digital Product Passport, was given real political fuel by the Burberry moment and everything that followed it in 2019.

If you're working in production, buying, or brand strategy right now, the concrete thing 2019 taught the industry is that your inventory disposal policy is a public document, whether you publish it or not. Someone will find out. Build your production quantities around what you can sell, donate, or genuinely recycle, not around what you can quietly destroy. The regulatory floor is rising and the consumer tolerance for the old approach is gone. Get ahead of it in your contracts today, not after the next story breaks.

Frequently asked questions

Why did Burberry burn unsold clothes and bags?

To protect brand exclusivity and prevent surplus goods from appearing in discount channels or grey markets, which would undermine the premium pricing strategy. It was standard practice across luxury, not unique to Burberry.

Did France actually ban fashion brands from destroying unsold goods?

Yes. The AGEC law, passed in early 2020 after being debated through 2019, banned the destruction of unsold non-food goods including textiles and clothing. It was the first law of its kind in a major economy.

What happened to Burberry after the backlash?

Burberry announced in late 2018 that it would stop destroying surplus goods and published sustainability commitments through 2019. However, independent verification of the change was limited, and the brand faced ongoing scrutiny about whether the policy shift was substantive or primarily a PR response.
