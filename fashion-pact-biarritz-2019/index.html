32 Fashion Giants Signed a Climate Pact in Biarritz. It Has No Teeth.

The Fashion Pact launched at the G7 in August 2019 with 32 companies and 150 brands. Here is what it commits to, and what it quietly leaves out.

The Fashion Pact that landed at the G7 summit in Biarritz on 26 August 2019 is a voluntary agreement, and that single word decides everything about whether it matters. I read the document on my phone in a fabric supplier's waiting room in Como the week it dropped, mostly because a mill manager I'd been arguing with about dyehouse effluent for two years texted me a screenshot with three question marks and nothing else. It runs to about two and a half pages. My honest read after a decade of sitting in sourcing meetings: this is the most useful thing the luxury sector has done on climate, and it still won't hit its own targets without something the pact deliberately avoids, which is a penalty.

Here's what's actually in it. Thirty-two companies representing roughly 150 brands, convened by Kering CEO Francois-Henri Pinault at Emmanuel Macron's request, committing to three buckets: climate, biodiversity, and oceans. Net zero by 2050, 100 percent renewable energy in owned operations by 2030, 25 percent low-impact materials by 2025, and phasing out single-use plastic in B2C packaging by 2030. Chanel, Adidas, H&M, Inditex, Nike, Hermes, Prada, Ralph Lauren, Stella McCartney, Salvatore Ferragamo. That's not a fringe coalition. That's most of the industry's revenue in one room.

What Got Signed in Biarritz, and What Got Left in the Draft

The pact is short on purpose. Anyone who has tried to get twelve competing brands to agree on a shared audit standard knows that every additional clause loses you three signatories. So the drafters, working with the Boston Consulting Group and building on the UN Fashion Industry Charter for Climate Action from 2018, wrote something broad enough to sign by August and specific enough to be embarrassing to miss.

The 2030 renewable energy target is the strongest line. It's measurable, it's inside company control, and it doesn't depend on a tier-four supplier in a country with an unreliable grid. The materials target is where I'd push back hardest. Twenty-five percent low-impact materials by 2025 sounds firm until you ask who defines low-impact. Recycled polyester counts. Recycled polyester is mostly PET bottles being pulled out of a functioning bottle-to-bottle recycling loop and downcycled into a shirt that will never be recycled again. It scores well on the metrics and does very little.

What isn't in the document: overproduction. Not a word about volume. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation put clothing production at roughly double what it was in 2000, with the average garment worn about 36 percent fewer times. You cannot decarbonise your way out of that with better cotton.

Why the Biodiversity Chapter Is the Sleeper

Most coverage this week has gone straight to the carbon numbers. The biodiversity commitments are the ones I'd watch, and I say that as someone who spent 2017 and 2018 trying to trace a single cashmere program back to grazing land in Inner Mongolia and failing three times before it worked.

Cashmere, wool, leather, viscose. Those four raw materials sit at the top of nearly every luxury supply chain and they're the ones where nobody can tell you where anything came from. When I finally got that cashmere trace to close, the answer was that our herder cooperative was overgrazing at roughly twice the sustainable stocking rate because the buying volumes we'd committed to made anything else impossible. We caused it. The pact's science-based biodiversity targets, if they get built properly, force that conversation into procurement rather than leaving it in the CSR report.

Quantis put the apparel and footwear industries at around 8 percent of global greenhouse gas impact in its 2018 study, and the large majority of that sits upstream in dyeing, finishing, and yarn preparation. Not in the flagship store's LED lighting.

The Enforcement Problem Everyone Is Being Polite About

There is no compliance mechanism. No third-party verification requirement written into the text, no exit penalty, no fine, no published scorecard tied to a deadline. A CEO can sign this in Biarritz and instruct nothing to change on Monday, and the only consequence is a journalist asking about it in 2026.

I'll be honest, I flip-flopped on this one. My friend Marta, who works in compliance at a mid-size Italian tannery and has approximately zero patience for any of this, told me over terrible coffee that voluntary pacts are just a way for big houses to write the rules before Brussels writes them for them, and I laughed and then didn't sleep well about it. She's probably half right? The counter-argument is that mandatory schemes take eight years to negotiate and this took eight months. I don't know how to weigh those against each other cleanly. Ask me in three years. For more on this, see The French Fashion Pact of 2019 Was Fashion's Most Serious Climate Commitment Yet.

What I'd bet on: regulation is coming regardless, and it will arrive as traceability mandates rather than emissions caps, because traceability is auditable and emissions self-reporting isn't. Brands that build real chain-of-custody data now will find themselves ahead when supply chain disclosure rules start biting, and the ones who treated the pact as a press release will be retrofitting under deadline.

What This Changes for Designers and Models Right Now

If you're a small label, the pact is not aimed at you and you should still read it, because your stockists will start asking pact-shaped questions within eighteen months. Three things that are actually actionable this season:

  • Get mill names on paper. Not agent names. Mill names, with addresses. If your supplier won't give them, that's your answer about what's upstream.
  • Stop buying recycled polyester as your sustainability story. Deadstock, mono-material construction, and lower unit volumes do more and cost less to substantiate.
  • Price the work properly. Sustainable sourcing dies in businesses that are undercapitalised, which is most of them. If cash flow is the thing blocking you, that's a solvable problem and fashion's money habits are fixable before your sourcing is.

For models and agencies, the relevant shift is slower but real. Casting briefs at the houses that signed will start carrying sustainability language, campaign shoots will get audited for travel and set waste, and the talent side will eventually get pulled into the same disclosure net.

Pick one raw material in your line this week, the one you buy most of, and write down the actual mill and the actual farm or forest it comes from. Not the trading company. If you can't complete that line in an afternoon of emails, you've found the gap the Fashion Pact is pointing at, and you've found it four years before anyone forces you to. That single traceability exercise will also position you for the supply chain data requirements EU regulators are already drafting, which will ask for the same information with a legal deadline attached.

Frequently asked questions

Who actually signed the Fashion Pact in 2019?

Thirty-two companies covering around 150 brands, including Chanel, Kering, H&M, Inditex, Adidas, Nike, Hermes, Prada and Ralph Lauren. Kering CEO Francois-Henri Pinault convened it at the request of French President Emmanuel Macron and presented it at the G7 summit in Biarritz on 26 August 2019.

Is the Fashion Pact legally binding?

No. It is a voluntary commitment with no fines, no exit penalty and no built-in third-party verification requirement. Accountability rests entirely on public reporting and reputational pressure.

What are the main targets in the Fashion Pact?

Net zero emissions by 2050, 100 percent renewable energy across owned operations by 2030, 25 percent low-impact materials by 2025, and eliminating single-use plastic in consumer packaging by 2030. It also includes science-based biodiversity targets and ocean commitments around microfibre pollution.
