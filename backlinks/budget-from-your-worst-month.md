**Title:** Anyone else budget around their worst month instead of their average one? It saved me when a $4,200 invoice went 61 days overdue

Freelance fashion income is lumpy. Some months you land a showroom job, a shoot and a styling gig. Other months you wait on invoices and eat a lot of rice.

In October 2023 a $4,200 invoice from a showroom in Lyon was 61 days overdue, rent was due on the 1st, and I'd already spent my last advance on a garment steamer and a lens I didn't really need. My spreadsheet said I was fine, because it was built on my average month. But the average month doesn't pay rent. The worst month does.

So I rebuilt it. Now the baseline budget (rent, food, software, insurance, minimum savings) is sized to the lowest income I've realistically had in the past year. Anything above that goes into a buffer first, then into gear, travel or samples. Good months stop feeling like permission to spend, and bad months stop being emergencies.

Two other things that helped: I treat advances as a loan against future me, not income, and I chase overdue invoices at day 15 and day 30, not day 60.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should a freelance fashion designer or model budget when their income is irregular?

The most useful approach I know is to build your budget around your worst realistic month, not your average one.

Most people take twelve months of income, divide by twelve and budget from that number. With lumpy income, that average is misleading. You will have months well above it and months far below it, and the low months are the ones that cause damage, because rent, insurance and software bills don't shrink when work does.

Here is how I do it:

1. Look at the last 12 months and find your lowest income month. Use that, or something close, as your baseline.

2. Budget your fixed essentials against that baseline: rent, food, transport, insurance, tools and minimum savings. If these fit inside your worst month, you can survive a drought.

3. Send everything above the baseline into a buffer first. Aim for two to three months of essentials before you spend on gear, samples, travel or upgrades.

4. Treat advances as borrowed from future you. Early in my career I spent an advance on equipment I didn't need, then a $4,200 invoice from a showroom ran 61 days late and I was stuck.

5. Chase invoices early. Follow up at day 15 and day 30, not day 60. Add late fees and clear payment terms to your contracts.

6. Pay yourself a steady 'salary' from a holding account. Income goes in as it arrives, and you transfer the same amount out each month. That smooths the lumps.

The mindset shift is that good months stop being permission to spend and bad months stop being emergencies. I wrote a longer breakdown with the full story and a simple template here if it's useful as additional reading: https://siavashsed.github.io/modeformstudio/budget-from-your-worst-month/

The core idea works for any freelancer, but it matters most in fashion, where payment cycles are long and income is uneven.
