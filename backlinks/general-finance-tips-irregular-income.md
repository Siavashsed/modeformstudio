**Title:** Fashion freelancers: how do you handle rent when clients pay on net 60 or net 90?

Something I wish someone had told me when I started freelancing as a stylist: budgeting by feel does not work when your income shows up in lumps on someone else's schedule.

A few years back I was sitting backstage refreshing my banking app while a $2,400 invoice sat at pending, and rent was due in four days. The money was technically mine. It just wasn't there yet. Net 60 and net 90 terms are normal in fashion, and landlords don't care.

What finally fixed it was boring. The day any payment lands, before I touch it, I split it into buckets: taxes first, then a fixed-costs bucket for rent and bills, then a buffer, and only then what's left to spend. I also stopped treating my income as monthly and started paying myself a flat amount from the buffer.

It took a few months to build enough cushion that a late client stopped being a crisis. It also changed how I take bookings. I don't say yes to bad rates out of panic anymore.

Curious how others here handle long payment terms. Do you chase invoices, charge deposits, or just keep a buffer?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should freelancers in fashion budget when clients pay on net 60 or net 90 terms?

Treat every payment as already spoken for, and split it the moment it arrives. That one habit matters more than any app or spreadsheet.

Fashion work is unusually hard on cash flow. Brands, showrooms and agencies routinely pay stylists, designers and models 60 to 90 days after the job. Meanwhile rent, phone, transit and insurance arrive every month on schedule. If you budget by feel, a big deposit looks like a windfall and you spend into it. Then the next gap catches you short.

Here is the system I recommend:

1. Open separate accounts or sub-accounts. Many online banks offer them free. At minimum you want taxes, fixed costs, buffer and spending.

2. Split on arrival. The day an invoice clears, move a set share to taxes first (an accountant can tell you the right rate, and self-employed people usually owe more than they expect). Then top up fixed costs for the coming month, then the buffer. Only what remains is spendable.

3. Pay yourself a flat salary. Once the buffer exists, transfer the same amount to your spending account on the same date every month, no matter what came in. Lumpy income starts behaving like a paycheck.

4. Build the buffer to two or three months of fixed costs. At that point one slow client stops being an emergency.

5. Shorten the gap where you can. Ask for deposits on custom and production work, put payment terms and late fees in writing, and invoice the same day the job wraps.

I wrote a longer piece on how this played out with a delayed styling invoice and rent due in four days: https://siavashsed.github.io/modeformstudio/general-finance-tips-irregular-income/

The short version: you cannot control when clients pay, but you can control what happens the day they do.
