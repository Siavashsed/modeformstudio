The 30% Rule That Saved My Fashion Career From Tax Season

General finance tips for models, stylists and designers paid in lumps: a cash buffer, a separate tax account and a fixed salary you pay yourself.

If you're paid in lumps, the best money advice I have is dull: pay yourself on a schedule you set, not on the day a check happens to clear. In March 2019 an agency owed me $4,100 for a Paris showroom booking, and that money reached my account 117 days later. By then I'd put two months of rent on a Visa at 24.99% APR. The interest came to about $190, roughly what I'd earned for a full day of fittings that season. So here's my position. Most finance advice is written for people with salaries, and if your income comes in bursts you need a different system.

The Federal Reserve's 2024 report on household economic well-being found that 63% of US adults would cover a surprise $400 expense with cash or its equivalent. I work with stylists, models and small-label designers, and for that crowd the number feels generous. People in this industry often earn well across a year and still get wiped out by one slow month. The fixes below are the ones I've watched hold up in real lives, my own included.

Build a Cash Buffer Before You Invest a Single Dollar

Before the Roth IRA, before the good industrial sewing machine, you need cash that just sits there. For anyone paid in lumps, a buffer covering three months of fixed costs does more than any other money move. I didn't believe that until I had one. The first January I went into a slow season with $6,200 in a plain savings account, I stopped taking panic bookings. My day rate went up the next season, because I could finally say no.

Count your costs, not your income

The standard advice is six months of income. I think that's wrong for creatives, and it scares people off before they start. Six months of a fashion income could mean $15,000 or $60,000, depending on the year. Your costs are a number you can actually find. Add up only what you'd still owe if the work stopped tomorrow:

  • Rent or mortgage, plus utilities
  • Health insurance premiums
  • Phone, studio space and software like Adobe Creative Cloud
  • Minimum debt payments
  • Groceries at a modest level, not your best-month level

Multiply that by three. For most people I've worked with it comes out somewhere between $7,000 and $14,000... which sounds enormous until you build it 10% of each check at a time.

Treat Tax Money Like It Was Never Yours

If you freelance in the US, nobody withholds tax for you. You owe 15.3% in self-employment tax, per the IRS, on top of regular income tax, and you're expected to pay it in quarterly installments. Skip those and the penalties build up quietly.

Move 25 to 30% of every payment into a separate tax account the day it clears. Don't wait for the end of the month. Some accountants will say 20% is plenty, and with good deductions in a low-tax state they could be right. I'd rather overshoot and get a pleasant April than the opposite. Put the four deadlines in your calendar: April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15. The details are on the IRS estimated taxes page. For more on this, see Fashion Freelancers Lose Thousands Every Season to One Tax Mistake.

Okay, confession. My tax account used to sit at the same bank as my checking, one tap away, and in 2021 I 'borrowed' $380 from it for a vintage Margiela coat at the Saint-Ouen flea market. I told myself I'd put it back by Friday. It went back in October. My friend Ines keeps her tax money at a completely different bank with no app on her phone, which honestly sounds a bit unhinged, but she's never short in April and I was. I'm not 100% sure the friction trick works for everyone... it worked on me, eventually.

Pay Yourself a Fixed Salary From Unpredictable Income

I've used this system since 2020. Every payment lands in one holding account. On the 1st and the 15th, I move the same fixed amount into checking, and that's my salary. Taxes come out first, then the buffer contribution, then the salary. Anything left stays in holding to cover the next lean month.

Plenty of people need a setup like this. Upwork's Freelance Forward 2023 report estimated that 64 million Americans freelanced that year, about 38% of the US workforce. Most budgeting apps still assume a paycheck every two weeks.

But here's the honest downside: this won't work in your first month. You need a few months of income built up in holding before a fixed salary is safe, and during that stretch you'll pay yourself less than feels fair. Set the salary at what your worst three-month stretch could support, not your best. Raise it twice a year if holding keeps growing. For more on the monthly cash crunch specifically, see how fashion creatives can stop scrambling every month.

Stop Calling Your Wardrobe a Business Expense

This part will annoy some readers. Fashion people are brilliant at calling purchases investments. The $1,100 boots are 'for castings' and the new bag is 'for client meetings.' I've told myself both of those stories. For more on this, see The Money Habit That Saved My First Fashion Collection.

The tax rule is narrower than most people think. According to IRS Publication 529, work clothes qualify only if you're required to wear them for work and they aren't suitable for everyday wear. In the well-known 1980 case Pevsner v. Commissioner, a manager at an Yves Saint Laurent boutique lost her deduction for YSL clothing she wore at work, because it could be worn anywhere. And if a boutique manager's YSL didn't count, your everyday blazer won't either.

Things that usually do hold up, at least in my experience:

  • Fabric, trims and samples for a collection
  • Comp cards, portfolio printing and test shoot costs
  • Agency fees and commissions
  • Travel to out-of-town castings and markets

Find a CPA who already has creative clients. Mine charges $450 a year and saves me more than that every April. If you want the wider picture, these finance tips most fashion professionals ignore cover the rest.

Before you close this tab, open a savings account at a different bank from your checking and name it TAX. Move 25% of your most recent payment into it, then set a calendar reminder for January 15, the next estimated payment deadline. That one transfer is the whole system in small form, and you can do it in about ten minutes.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a freelance model or stylist save for taxes?

Set aside 25 to 30% of every payment in a separate account the day it clears. That covers the 15.3% self-employment tax plus federal and state income tax for most people. A CPA can lower that figure once they know your deductions.

How big should my emergency fund be if my income is irregular?

Aim for three months of fixed costs, not six months of income. Add up rent, insurance, phone, subscriptions, minimum debt payments and basic groceries, then multiply by three. For most people in fashion that lands between $7,000 and $14,000.

Can I deduct clothes I buy for photo shoots or castings?

Only if the clothing is required for the work and isn't suitable for everyday wear. Pieces you could wear to dinner almost never qualify, even if you bought them for a casting. Costumes, specialized wardrobe and samples for a collection have a much better chance.
