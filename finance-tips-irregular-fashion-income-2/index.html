The 97-Day Invoice That Rewrote Every Money Rule I Had

General finance tips built for irregular fashion income: a fake salary, a separate tax account, and a safety net sized to your slowest season.

Most general finance tips fail creative workers for one reason: they assume a paycheck shows up every two weeks. Fix the timing problem first, and the rest of the standard advice starts working. In March 2019 I shipped a capsule collection to a Paris showroom, invoiced €4,200, and watched that money land 97 days later, by which point two months of studio rent were sitting on a Visa charging 24.99%. I wasn't bad with money. Timing was the problem, and nobody had ever told me those are different things.

I've spent the years since running a small studio and booking freelance fit modeling on the side, and I've watched the same pattern play out in dozens of designers and models I've worked with. On paper, the income is fine. Cash flow is a mess. So everything below is built around the gaps between payments, not around a pretty monthly budget template.

Pay Yourself a Boring Fake Salary

The change that fixed my finances wasn't a budget. It was a second checking account. Every payment, whether it's a design fee or a fitting day, lands in one holding account, and on the first of each month I move the same fixed amount into the account I actually spend from. Your spending account should never know whether you had a good month.

To set the number, add up what you earned over the last 12 months after tax, divide by 12, and cut 15% off the top. In 2021 mine came to $3,100. It felt tight. That was also the first year I didn't touch a credit card for rent, because the lean months were already paid for by the fat ones.

The catch is getting started. You need roughly one month's salary sitting in the holding account before the system runs itself, and it took me about five months of living lean to build that. If you're in that rough patch right now, I wrote about staying solvent between seasons in more detail.

Your Tax Money Was Never Yours

If you freelance in the US, you owe self-employment tax on top of income tax. That's 15.3% for Social Security and Medicare, according to the IRS, and it catches nearly every first-year freelancer I've met. Since nobody withholds it for you, you're expected to send quarterly estimated payments in April, June, September and January.

My rule: the same day a payment clears, 25 to 30% goes to a savings account at a different bank. A different bank matters. When the money sits one tap away, you'll "borrow" it, and I did exactly that in 2020 to cover a $1,800 fabric order. Paying it back took until the following spring, plus an underpayment penalty I'm still annoyed about. For more on this, see The Money Rules I Wish Someone Told Me Before My First Fashion Week.

So the full setup looks like this:

  • Holding account: every payment lands here first.
  • Tax account, separate bank: 25 to 30% of each deposit, moved the same day.
  • Spending account: your fixed monthly salary and nothing else.
  • Slow-season buffer: the reserve that carries you through dead months.

Size Your Safety Net to Your Worst Season, Not a Rule of Thumb

The standard advice says keep three to six months of expenses saved. I think that's the wrong measure for anyone in fashion. Look back over the last two years, find your longest stretch with little or no income, and add one month. For a lot of designers that's the gap between delivering a collection and getting paid for it, which can run well past 90 days.

Most people are starting from a thin cushion. The Federal Reserve's report on the economic well-being of US households found that only 63% of adults in 2023 would cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent. Models often have it worse. New York's Fashion Workers Act, pushed through with help from the Model Alliance, now requires management companies to pay talent within 45 days, which helps... but only in one state, and only if you know to chase it.

My friend Inès, who walks shows and does a ton of e-commerce work, tried the cash envelope method last year. Actual envelopes, labeled in Sharpie, in a shoebox under her bed. By October the "taxes" envelope held a Post-it that said "IOU 340, taxis" and nothing else. I laughed at her for a week, then admitted I'd done the digital version of the same thing in 2020. Honestly, I'm not sure envelopes work for anyone whose income shows up in random lumps. Maybe they do. My mother swears by them, but she had a salary for 31 years.

Kill Card Debt Before You Invest a Single Dollar

I'll take a firm line here. If you carry a credit card balance, paying it down beats investing almost every time. The Fed's G.19 consumer credit release has shown average rates on interest-charging accounts above 21% for most of the past two years, and no index fund reliably returns that. Using a card to float yourself between invoices feels clever... it's a slow leak.

People genuinely argue over whether to clear the highest rate first or the smallest balance first. The math favors the highest rate. But I've seen the small-balance approach work better for people who need a quick win to stay motivated, and a plan you stick with beats a perfect one you quit in month two.

Once the cards are clear, open a SEP IRA or a Solo 401(k) and fill it with boring, low-cost index funds. That's it. I covered the retirement side more in the finance habits most fashion professionals ignore, and if the monthly scramble is still your biggest problem, start with this piece on fixing the monthly cash crunch. For more on this, see A 94 Day Late Invoice Taught Me More Than Any Budget App.

Before you close this tab, open a savings account at a bank you don't already use and name it "Taxes." The next time a client pays you, move 25% of that payment there the same day. Repeat that one transfer every time and it'll do more for you than any budgeting app I've ever downloaded.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a freelancer set aside for taxes from each payment?

Most US freelancers should move 25 to 30% of each payment into a separate account the day it clears. That covers federal income tax plus the 15.3% self-employment tax. High earners or people in high-tax states may need closer to 35%.

How big should my emergency fund be if my income is irregular?

Find your longest stretch of low or no income over the past two years and add one month. For many designers and models that works out to somewhere between four and eight months of expenses, more than the usual three-month rule suggests.

Should I pay off credit cards or start investing first?

Pay off the cards first if they charge 20% or more, and most do. No index fund reliably beats that rate. Once the balance is gone, send the same monthly payment into a SEP IRA or Solo 401(k).
