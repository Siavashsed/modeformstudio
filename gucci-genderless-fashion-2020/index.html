Gucci Went Genderless in 2020 and Fashion Had No Good Excuse Left

Gucci's 2020 genderless runway shift forced fashion to reckon with binary design. Here's what it meant, what it cost, and what actually changed.

Gucci merged its men's and women's shows into a single, unified collection in 2020, and the industry's reaction told you everything about where fashion actually stood on gender. I was at the Gucci Epilogue presentation in July 2020, watching the live stream from my studio in Paris at around 2am because the time zones weren't kind, and I remember the specific feeling of watching models in fluid silk trousers and structured corset pieces walk together without any of the usual gendered segmentation. No separate invitations. No separate press notes. One collection, one vision, Alessandro Michele refusing to pretend the old categories still made sense.

The move wasn't made in a vacuum. It landed in the middle of a pandemic that had already ripped the fashion calendar apart, with houses scrambling to justify the cost and carbon footprint of flying press and buyers to seasonal shows. Michele used that disruption deliberately. He'd been building toward this for years, dressing Harry Styles in ruffled blouses and putting men in floral suits long before 2020, but the formal merger of the shows was a structural declaration. It changed how the brand presented product, how buyers ordered, and how the rest of the industry would need to explain itself when asked why they still kept the categories separate.

Why the 2020 Calendar Collapse Made This Possible

The traditional fashion calendar was already under serious pressure before COVID arrived. Smaller brands had been pushing back against the twice-yearly treadmill for a few years, and Business of Fashion had been running the argument about calendar reform since at least 2016. But it took a global shutdown to give the bigger houses permission to actually act. When the Milan spring 2021 shows were being planned in mid-2020, several major labels were working with essentially no confirmed physical format and budgets that had been slashed after Q1 retail collapses.

Gucci's response was the Epilogue series, a 12-hour live streaming event filmed inside their Rome offices. No audience. No transport. Models photographed themselves using Polaroid cameras in an act that was either genuinely radical or very expensive performance art, depending on how charitable you were feeling. Either way, it worked as media. The coverage was enormous, and the format cost a fraction of a traditional runway production.

The structural point matters here: by collapsing the men's and women's shows, Gucci cut two productions to one. That's real money. The average cost of a major runway show at that point sat somewhere between $200,000 and $1 million depending on scale and location, according to industry estimates cited by Vogue. Michele framed the decision in terms of creative philosophy, and I believe he meant it, but the financial logic was sitting right underneath that philosophy the whole time.

What the Genderless Pivot Actually Required from Designers

Calling a collection genderless is easy. Designing one that actually works across a range of bodies, that fits, that doesn't just mean 'oversized on everyone,' is genuinely hard. I've worked with pattern cutters on androgynous capsule pieces and the fitting process is twice as long. You're not designing for one set of proportions. You're solving for shoulder width, hip-to-waist ratio, and inseam length across a much wider range than any single gendered fit chart accounts for. Most brands that claimed genderless collections in 2020 were actually just making women's pieces in larger sizes or men's pieces with softer fabrication. That's not genderless. That's lazy sizing with a press release.

Gucci's approach was more considered. Michele built around silhouettes that were deliberately not anchored to conventional body proportions: wide-leg cuts, layered volumes, structured pieces that didn't rely on a defined waist. This is the design strategy that actually holds up. McKinsey's State of Fashion 2021 report, which captured 2020 industry shifts, noted that genderless or gender-fluid product lines had grown by roughly 40% in major retailer assortments over 2019-2020, though the report was careful to distinguish between genuine design intent and marketing repositioning.

The design challenge also touches something that doesn't get discussed enough: fit grading for genderless collections has no established industry standard. Every brand is essentially making its own rules, which means customers can't rely on their usual size knowledge when buying across houses. That's a real problem for adoption at scale, and it hasn't been solved yet.

The Industry Reaction Was Admiring and Mostly Cowardly

The critical response to Gucci's 2020 move was warm. Effusively warm, in that way that fashion press sometimes uses praise as a way of avoiding the harder question, which is: why haven't you done this? The coverage celebrated Michele's vision without asking many of the labels sitting in the audience whether they planned to follow. Most didn't, at least not structurally. A few brands launched 'unisex' capsules. Saint Laurent quietly kept its gendered show format. Prada waited.

Okay, I'll be honest here for a second. I had a conversation with my friend Teri, who works wholesale for a mid-tier European ready-to-wear brand, and she said something that stuck with me: 'Our buyers don't know how to rack it.' She meant that the physical retail infrastructure, the way stock gets organized, purchased, and displayed in department stores and boutiques, is still entirely built around a men's floor and a women's floor. Genderless collections create a genuine logistical problem that nobody in the press was writing about in 2020. Where does it go? Who buys it at the trade show? I don't have a clean answer for that and I'm not sure anyone does yet.

The brands that handled the moment best were the ones that had already built their identity around fluidity before it became a conversation. Palomo Spain, Wales Bonner, Telfar. They weren't pivoting. They were just continuing. That distinction matters when you're evaluating who was actually serious about the shift and who was responding to a news cycle. The transparency gap between those two groups was obvious in 2020, and it connects to the broader accountability questions the industry was starting to face around supply chain and product claims, questions that have only gotten louder since. If you're curious about where that transparency pressure is heading at a structural level, the EU's Digital Product Passport initiative gives a useful window into how regulators are thinking about it.

What Changed, What Didn't, and What This Year Actually Proved

By the end of 2020, two major houses had formally merged their seasonal shows. Several more had restructured their presentation calendars in ways that blurred the gendered show schedule. Statista reported that the global apparel market contracted by around 20% in 2020 due to pandemic conditions, which meant every decision about show format and collection structure had a financial pressure attached to it that wouldn't have existed in a normal year. The genderless shift and the calendar reform happened together, and it's worth being honest that adversity accelerated what advocacy had only nudged.

But here's the thing... the change that felt structural in the summer of 2020 had softened considerably by spring 2021. Separate show invitations came back. Buyers started requesting gendered lookbooks again because their retail systems hadn't changed. The cultural conversation moved forward while the operational infrastructure stayed put. That gap is the real story of what 2020 meant for genderless fashion: a genuine crack in the wall, not the wall coming down.

And that's worth sitting with. Gucci's decision was real and the design work was serious. Michele wasn't performing. But a brand with Gucci's resources and identity can absorb the operational complexity of genderless presentation in ways a mid-market brand simply can't. The move proved the concept was viable at the top of the market. It left open the harder question of how the rest of the industry follows without the same budget, the same brand equity, or the same buyer relationships. That question is still open. The same structural gap between a brand's public commitments and its actual supply chain accountability that Burberry's stock destruction scandal exposed in 2019 shows up here too, just wearing different clothes.

If you're designing or buying for a brand that wants to move toward gender-fluid product, start with your fit grading before you touch your marketing. Audit your current size range, map the actual body data you're fitting to, and find where your patterns break when you remove the gendered assumption. The press release can wait. The pattern can't.

Frequently asked questions

Did Gucci actually stop doing separate men's and women's shows in 2020?

Yes. Alessandro Michele formally merged Gucci's men's and women's collections into a single presentation starting in 2020, beginning with the Epilogue series filmed in Rome. The decision was framed as a permanent creative shift, not a pandemic stopgap.

What does genderless fashion actually mean for how clothes are designed?

It means designing silhouettes and fit standards that work across a wider range of body proportions without assuming a gendered baseline. In practice this usually means avoiding fitted waist seams, building in size range flexibility, and grading patterns differently than conventional menswear or womenswear blocks.

Did other fashion houses follow Gucci's lead on genderless collections after 2020?

A few did, at least partially. Brands like Palomo Spain and Wales Bonner had already built gender-fluid identities before 2020. Most major houses made smaller moves, launching unisex capsules or adjusting presentation formats, but kept their core gendered show structure intact by 2021.
